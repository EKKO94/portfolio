I designed two ways to ask for help in IoT Unified: a dedicated assistant for broader questions and a panel beside node configuration.

The need

Guidance through a complex, data-rich workspace.

My design

A dedicated assistant and help within node configuration.

The outcome

In use at Sorba.ai. Two assistant surfaces designed in about two months; next, test with industrial users.

Problem & scope Design decisions Outcome & next steps

Context

Helping users navigate technical complexity.

IoT Unified brings together a large volume of data and many configurable nodes. The company wanted an assistant for questions about node setup and configuration. The design needed to offer help while leaving room for the task itself.

The project manager defined the requirements together with the heads of the development department.

My contribution

  • Reviewed conversational interface patterns and adapted them to IoT Unified.
  • Designed the dedicated workspace and embedded assistant panel.
  • Designed model selection, starter actions and prompt suggestions.

I reviewed proposals with developers from unrelated projects. They were peer reviewers, not the industrial users who would configure nodes. Direct user access was unavailable, so I could not validate their needs or working habits firsthand.

This case covers the interface design: how people ask questions, choose a model and are prompted to verify responses.

Pattern research

Learning from familiar AI interfaces.

I explored ChatGPT, Gemini, DeepSeek and other AI products as references, then iterated on what could fit IoT Unified. This was a review of interface patterns, separate from research with end users.

  • ChatGPT
  • Gemini
  • DeepSeek

The design questions: how should someone begin a conversation, understand the available controls and choose a model? I adapted those patterns to questions about industrial data and configuration.

What I adapted

A familiar message composer, task-oriented starting points, a visible model selector and a reminder to verify responses.

Design decisions

Two places to ask for help.

I gave broader questions a dedicated workspace and put focused help beside node configuration. That second entry point keeps the form nearby, but it also competes for space.

The trade-off: conversation space or task context

Dedicated assistant only Hypothetical alternative
Gives the conversation more room, but asking about a field means moving away from its configuration screen.
Dedicated + embedded Design shown
Keeps help close to the form. The panel can obscure fields, and users must understand when to use each entry point.

Retrospective comparison, not a record of tested alternatives. Whether both entry points are needed remains a validation question.

Decision 02 — Make the model choice visible

The user chooses the model.

The design exposes more than one model. I kept the selector visible in the dedicated workspace and used an explicit list of names, so the choice is available without opening settings.

Visibility does not explain which model fits a task. I would test whether people can make that choice, or need descriptions and a recommended default.

Model options shown in the design. No performance ranking is implied.

Decision 03 — Give users a way to begin

Turn a blank prompt into a starting point.

The home offers starter actions, while this exploration suggests questions about channels, flows, smart rules, alerts and permissions. I used the language of IoT Unified to make the blank composer less abstract.

Each suggestion also sets an expectation about what Sorbot can answer. Those prompts still need to be checked against the assistant’s actual capabilities.

Decision 04 — Set expectations about AI responses

Guidance still needs verification.

I included a reminder to verify Sorbot’s responses. An answer should be checked before it informs a configuration change. Next, I would test whether users can verify an answer before changing configuration.

In-product reminder

SORBOT isn’t perfect—it’s a good idea to verify its responses.

The interface also shows what Sorbot is doing: it lists its steps while it thinks, ends answers with their sources and says so when the connection drops.

Iteration & peer review

Reviewing beyond the project team.

The project took approximately two months. Company practice did not give me direct access to real users in the industrial sector, so I invited developers from other, unrelated projects to review the design as I iterated.

That gave me an outside perspective on the interface. It did not replace validation with people configuring nodes in their day-to-day work.

Reviewer feedback → design changes

REVIEW 1

Show where answers come from

Raised

The reviewers asked where the information in an answer comes from.

Changed

Answers end with numbered sources and a Learn more row of linked sites with their icons.

REVIEW 2

Icons instead of names

Raised

The reviewers asked for icons instead of names to choose where Sorbot searches, and for other controls.

Changed

Each source first showed a short name next to its icon, which worked while there were few. As more were added, I kept only the icons, as ChatGPT and Claude do. The menu opens to each source’s name, a short description, its icon and a toggle.

Design outcome & next steps

Two ways to ask.
Next: validate with users.

The design work brings together a dedicated assistant, chat within a node flow, model selection and starting prompts.

Sorbot is in use at Sorba.ai, and anyone can try it through the Sorba trial, which is limited until a paid plan. I no longer have access to the product, so I have no usage data to share.

Configuration success, completion time and support needs are the outcomes to evaluate next with end users.

What I would test next

Proposed tasks. I would use representative configuration questions and check the answers independently.

  1. Find help during setup

    Ask someone to resolve a configuration question. Watch which entry point they choose and whether the panel blocks fields they need.

  2. Explain the model choice

    Ask them to identify the active model and explain what they expect from another option. Look for uncertainty before adding more controls.

  3. Check an answer before editing

    In a test environment, observe whether they can spot an incomplete answer, verify it and make a deliberate change. Record where the interface gives too little help.

Reflection

A familiar chat UI is only the starting point.

My next priority is to test how users choose a model and verify an answer during configuration.

I would bring industrial users in earlier and test one configuration task end to end before expanding the interface. That would help me decide which prompts, model controls and response states earn their place.
